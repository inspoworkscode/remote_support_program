Investment proposal · September 2026

We built the tool our own IT company needed. Now we sell it to every other one.

Every IT support company in the country pays a foreign software firm every month, per technician, just to connect to a customer's computer. Then it pays again for something to track the jobs. We built one program that does both — and we own every line of it.

The problem, in plain English

Small IT companies are paying twice, in dollars, for tools that were never built for them.

They pay per person

The big remote-control programs charge for each technician. A three-man shop pays three times. Prices are in dollars, so every time the rand slips, the bill goes up.

They pay again for the paperwork

Connecting to a customer's PC is half the job. The other half — who phoned, what was wrong, what was done, what to invoice — needs a second system, bought separately.

Nothing joins up

The remote tool knows nothing about the customer. The job system knows nothing about the machine. The technician retypes everything, twice, on every call.

What we built

One program. It fixes the computer and runs the business around it.

A technician clicks a name and is on the customer's screen. Everything that happens is already attached to that customer's record — no retyping, no second program, no second bill.

It does everything the others do

Take control of the mouse and keyboard. Work on password and permission screens the others often can't reach. Send files and whole folders both ways. Copy and paste between the two machines. Hear what the customer's computer is playing. Chat to the person sitting there.

And then it does the part they don't

Job cards for walk-ins, workshop repairs, call-outs and remote work. Customer records. A list of every machine, with its age, memory and disk space. And a health check that runs itself every month and emails the customer a report with your logo on it.

The part that sells it: we charge R500 a month for a whole company of up to five technicians — not per person. The same three-man shop currently pays several times that, in dollars, and still has no job cards.

Why this is not a slide deck with a dream attached

It is built, and each piece was tested until it proved itself.

Software is easy to claim and hard to check. So every feature below was verified by a separate test that had to pass before we accepted it — not by someone looking at a screen and saying it seemed fine.

Working

Controlling the customer's computer. Mouse, keyboard, several monitors, and the permission prompts that stop most remote tools dead. Proven on real customer machines.

Working

Unattended access. The technician connects to a machine with nobody sitting at it — the after-hours work that keeps a business running.

Working

Files and folders, both directions. Tested by sending a folder, then having the customer's machine independently fingerprint every file that arrived and comparing. Nineteen checks out of nineteen. A folder of files landed in under a third of a second.

Working

Hearing the customer's machine. Built and confirmed over a live connection to a real customer PC. The program also tells the technician why a machine is silent — which speaker it is using, whether it is muted. Neither TeamViewer nor AnyDesk does that.

Working

Surviving a dropped connection. If the technician's internet blinks, the session waits ninety seconds and picks up where it left off instead of collapsing. Ten checks out of ten against a real machine.

Working

The monthly health check. Runs itself on the customer's machine, writes a report in your colours with your logo, and emails it. Already sent for real, with the report attached.

Working

The job system and customer portal. Job cards, customer records, machines grouped by customer, appointments for call-outs, workshop repairs with serial numbers. In daily use.

Needs the money

The digital signature. Without it, Windows shows a scary warning the first time a customer installs the program. It is a certificate we buy, not something we can code around — and it is the single thing most likely to lose a sale.

Needs the money

Proper hosting and encryption. Today it runs on our own network for our own testing. Going public means paid servers, a security certificate and monitoring.

Needs the money

Mac, iPhone and Android. Windows is done. The others are a known, ordinary piece of work — they just need paying for.

What you get back

Income from month one of trading, and a share of the business if it is ever sold.

A monthly dividend

Ten percent of everything the product turns over, paid to you every month. Not ten percent of profit — of turnover, before any costs are taken out. It starts the first month a customer pays and does not stop.

Ten percent of a sale

Software businesses with monthly subscriptions are bought by larger companies at a multiple of their yearly income. If that day comes, a tenth of the price is yours.

Put plainly: at 250 customers your share is R150 000 a year against a R500 000 investment — and it keeps paying every year after that, on top of your share of any sale.

Straight answers

The three questions you should be asking.

What stops TeamViewer from simply adding job cards?

Nothing, except twenty years of not doing it. Their business is selling licences per technician to large corporates; the small IT shop's paperwork is not a market they chase. Even if they did, they would be adding it to a product that costs several times ours per person.

What if nobody buys it?

One company already has. Anywhere IT will run its own 1 000 clients on it in month one, replacing software it currently pays for. The product has a paying-scale user before a single stranger is approached — which is also why the pilot in months 2 to 4 is with two firms who did not build it. If they will not use it, we find that out in month three, not year three.

What if something happens to the developer?

Nothing in this is licensed or rented from anyone — the code is ours outright, so no supplier can switch it off or put the price up. And it is documented as it is built: every decision, every trap, and the reason behind each one is written down in the project itself, specifically so another developer can pick it up.
